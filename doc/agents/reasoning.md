# Reasoning and reflection norms

The problem should first be understood through its objects, assumptions,
dependencies, and intended outputs. The choice of method should follow from
that understanding rather than precede it.

Before introducing a construction, identify the difficulty it resolves. A
technique should not be included merely because it is available or conventional.
Seek a representation that exposes the relevant structure and makes the
required operations possible. Avoid unnecessary intermediate problems, and
justify abstraction by what it clarifies, preserves, or enables.

Distinguish the roles of geometry, constitutive assumptions, prescribed data,
coupled unknowns, and numerical choices. Track explicit and implicit
dependencies separately. After obtaining a result, identify its interpretation
and consequences; justify the passage from a mathematical property to a
modelling or computational conclusion.

When comparing cases, identify what the comparison isolates: quantities held
fixed, quantities changed, and remaining confounding effects. Agreement in one
observable is not agreement of complete models. Model adequacy is relative to
a specified quantity, measure, and regime. Keep differences, errors relative
to a reference, and validation against observations distinct.

Important calculations should be checked through an independent route when one
is available. Limiting cases, alternative formulations, and direct numerical
evaluations are appropriate checks, but a check must not be reported unless it
has actually been performed.

Reflection should target specific possible failures. Associate each objection
with the assumption it challenges and the conclusion it affects; evidence for
one limitation must not be used to dismiss another. Test every proposed
simplification against the complete setting. An algebraic cancellation is not
the absence of indirect dependence, and a property at fixed inputs cannot be
extended to coupled unknowns without justification.

Seek a simple organizing explanation, but test its scope rather than protecting
it. When it exceeds the evidence, revise it. Explain anomalies to their root
cause before treating them as findings or using them to motivate changes.

The final exposition should present the necessary assumptions, decisive
arguments, checks, interpretations, and limitations. It need not reproduce the
chronology of discovery or every exploratory attempt.
